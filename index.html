      The fine print

      Disclaimer

      Who Ruled Earth is an educational visualization. It shows one scholarly reading of the past, drawn as clean lines on a map. The real past was messier.

      Borders are approximate

      • For most of history, borders were frontiers and zones of influence rather than surveyed lines. Sharp edges here are a simplification.
      • The data is sampled at varying time steps, so borders jump between snapshots rather than changing smoothly year by year. A shape can be decades out of date at any given year.
      • Shapes have been simplified for the web and can be off by tens of kilometers, especially along coasts and small islands.
      • Blank areas mean no state is recorded in the dataset. People lived there; they may have been organized in ways a map of states doesn't capture.

      Cities are estimates

      City populations are historians' estimates, often rough and sometimes centuries apart. A city appears only around years with an estimate, so a missing city may simply lack data. Names follow the period for well-known renamings (Constantinople, Edo, Leningrad); otherwise the modern name is used.

      Not a statement on any dispute

      Names, dates and territories follow the Cliopatria dataset. Showing them is not an endorsement of any claim, past or present, and nothing here should be used to argue current territorial, legal or political questions.

      Errors and corrections

      Spot a mistake? Corrections to the underlying data go to the Seshat Global History Databank, which reviews them with historians. See for links.

      Privacy

      Every visit is counted by Vercel Web Analytics, which uses no cookies and doesn't identify you: it records the page, a rough location and your browser type.

      When you open a territory's details, your browser asks Wikipedia and Wikidata for its summary, rulers and events. Quiz results are saved only in your browser.

      If you click "Accept all empires", the site also loads Google Analytics, which sets cookies to count visits and see how the map is used. If you choose "No thanks" or close the note, Google Analytics never loads. Either way, your browser stores one small flag so the note stays closed. There are no ads, and nothing is sold. Clicking a Wikipedia or Wikidata link takes you to those sites, which have their own policies.

      Resources & sources

      Border data

      • Cliopatria: the political borders on this map, covering 1,600+ polities from 3400 BCE to 2024 CE. Licensed CC BY 4.0; converted to TopoJSON and trimmed for the web. Seshat Global History Databank
      • Bennett et al., "Cliopatria – A geospatial database of world-wide political entities from 3400BCE to 2024CE" Scientific Data 12, 247 (2025)
      • Seshat World Map: the dataset's home, with polity pages and a way to report corrections.
      • Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 license

      Cities

      • Reba, Reitsma & Seto, "Spatializing 6,000 years of global urbanization from 3700 BC to AD 2000": the cities and population estimates, digitized from Tertius Chandler and George Modelski. Licensed CC BY 4.0; merged and lightly cleaned for this map. Scientific Data 3, 160034 (2016)

      Base map and code

      • Natural Earth: the land outline (public domain), via the world-atlas package.
      • D3 and TopoJSON: projection and drawing.
      • Each territory links to its Wikipedia article and Wikidata entry. Summaries come live from Wikipedia; capitals, rulers and key events come live from Wikidata, so how much appears depends on how complete each entry is.

      Explore further

      • OpenHistoricalMap: a community-edited historical map with its own time slider and very fine local detail in places.
      • historical-basemaps: world boundary snapshots for selected years, including cultural regions.
      • David Rumsey Map Collection: tens of thousands of scanned historical maps.
      • Ancient World Mapping Center (UNC): maps and data for the ancient Mediterranean world.
